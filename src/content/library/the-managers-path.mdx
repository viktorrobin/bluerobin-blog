## The one-paragraph verdict

Fournier structures the book as a literal climb — mentor, tech lead, manager, manager-of-managers, director, VP — and each chapter is honest about what actually changes at that level instead of repeating generic leadership advice. The strength is specificity: the one-on-one templates, the "what a skip-level is for" framing, and the real anecdotes from Rent the Runway and Goldman are immediately usable. The fair criticism is that it leans toward larger, well-funded orgs, so the upper chapters (director, VP) read more like reconnaissance than instructions if you're at a small shop, and the earliest mentoring chapter is thinner than the rest. Even so, no other book covers the *whole* ladder this clearly, and the per-rung structure means it stays useful for years.

## Who should read it

New tech leads and first-time managers should read the relevant chapter the week they get the role. Senior ICs weighing the jump get an unsentimental preview of what they'd actually be trading away. Skip it only if you want deep coaching technique — this is a map, not a manual on the conversations themselves.

## Where it earned its place

The "read one level ahead" idea runs straight through [the leadership essays](/leadership/) — most of them started as notes I took managing against Fournier's rungs. Her chapter on what a manager-of-managers owes the layer below shaped how I argued span-of-control in [sizing teams in the copilot era](/leadership/sizing-teams-copilot-era/), where the question is exactly how many reports stay viable when tooling changes the work.

## Skip it if…

You run a five-person startup and want tactics for *today* — the director and VP chapters will feel like someone else's problem, and you'd get more mileage from a book on early-stage team-building.
