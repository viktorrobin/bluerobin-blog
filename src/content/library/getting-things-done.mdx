## The one-paragraph verdict

The premise holds up: your brain is for having ideas, not storing them, and an externalized capture-and-review system removes a surprising amount of background stress. That mechanism is the book's lasting contribution, and it works. The execution is the problem — Allen pads a clean methodology across far too many pages, and the tooling advice (label makers, tickler files, physical inboxes) is visibly stuck in a pre-smartphone era. The weekly review is the habit that actually makes GTD click, and it's underweighted relative to the filing minutiae. Read it for the model, not the implementation.

## Who should read it

Anyone running on memory and willpower to track commitments — engineers especially, since the open-loop problem is exactly what wrecks deep work. If you already keep a trusted task system and review it regularly, you've absorbed the lesson and can skip the book. The methodology matters more than the man's specific tools.

## Where it earned its place

It gave me one durable habit: a weekly review where everything gets captured, clarified, and put somewhere I trust. That single ritual outlasted every app I've cycled through since. The book earns its shelf spot on the strength of one idea executed in my own life, not on its prose.

## Skip it if…

Skip it if you bounce off self-help padding, or if you want a modern, tool-specific playbook — this isn't that. Read a solid GTD summary instead and you'll lose almost nothing.
