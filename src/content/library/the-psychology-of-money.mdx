## The one-paragraph verdict

Nineteen short, self-contained essays arguing that how you behave with money matters more than what you know about it. The strongest chapters — on luck versus risk, "enough," and compounding patience — are genuinely good and stuck with me. The recurring criticism is fair: it's written for people who consider themselves novices, it's anecdote-heavy rather than rigorous, and a few ideas get restated until they wear thin. There's no portfolio formula here, by design. What you get instead is a clean mental model you can actually apply.

## Who should read it

Engineers who happily tune a database for a week but have never looked at their own savings rate. Anyone who treats money as a pure optimization problem and keeps losing to their own impulses. Skip it if you already read behavioral-finance literature — you'll find it thin — or if you wanted concrete tactics, allocations, or numbers.

## Where it earned its place

It reframed wealth as the spending you don't see, and "enough" as a number you have to define before the market defines it for you. That stuck. It's the rare finance book I'd hand to a smart friend who finds the topic boring — short chapters, no jargon, real ideas.

## Skip it if…

You want depth, math, or a system. This is mindset, not method, and it repeats itself.
