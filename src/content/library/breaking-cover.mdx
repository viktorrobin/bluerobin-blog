## The one-paragraph verdict

Assad writes with the thing memoirs usually fake: actual self-awareness. The strongest passages are the unglamorous ones — recruiting sources, sitting in a war zone, the grinding ambiguity of deciding whether someone is lying to you. Where reviewers consistently praise the honesty and dry humor, I'd agree. Where they push back — that the book leans hard into faith and personal calling, and stays vague on the operational detail you came for — they're right too. It's a fast read that left me wanting the version with twenty percent more spycraft and ten percent less reflection.

## Who should read it

Read it if you like first-person accounts of intelligence work and you're more interested in the human texture of the job than the gadgets. The faith-and-purpose framing is load-bearing, not decorative — if that's a draw, this is your book; if it's a turnoff, you'll feel it on every other page. Skip it if you wanted a granular operational history.

## Where it earned its place

This one is purely off-duty reading — no platform tie-in, and I won't pretend otherwise. What it gave me was a useful reminder that most consequential work is judgment under incomplete information, made calmly, with people you can't fully trust. That mindset travels well past espionage.
