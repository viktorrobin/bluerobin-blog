## The one-paragraph verdict

Meyer's core move is to stop treating "communication problems" as personality clashes and instead plot them on eight scales — trust, disagreement, feedback, decision-making, and so on. The framework is genuinely good: it's concrete, it's relative (German vs. Japanese, not German vs. some imagined neutral), and the real-world anecdotes make each scale memorable. The fair criticism — which I share — is that it generalizes nations into tidy positions, and if you read it carelessly you walk away with new stereotypes instead of new sensitivity. It can also feel repetitive once you've internalized the pattern around chapter four. Read it for the vocabulary, not as a rulebook.

## Who should read it

Anyone leading a team that spans more than one country should read it, full stop — it pays for itself the first time you reframe a "blunt" colleague as high on the negative-feedback scale rather than rude. ICs who travel or work cross-border will get value too. Skip it if your team is monocultural and co-located; the payoff scales with the distance and diversity you're managing.

## Where it earned its place

The feedback and disagreement scales went straight into how I structured remote teams in [Org design across continents](/leadership/org-design-across-continents-distributed-teams/). Naming where each region sat on the directness axis turned a recurring source of friction — written reviews reading as harsh to some, vague to others — into a calibration we could actually discuss out loud.

## Skip it if…

Pass if you want rigorous social science — this is pattern-language from a practitioner, not peer-reviewed research, and treating its national averages as gospel will steer you wrong with any individual person.
