## The one-paragraph verdict

Brooks reports rather than preaches, and that is the whole appeal — twelve long New Yorker pieces on the Edsel, an insider-trading scandal, a corporate-secrets lawsuit, each built from interviews and court records instead of frameworks. The prose is genuinely good: dry, precise, no jargon, which is rarer in this genre than it should be. What he is actually documenting is how people behave under money and pressure, and that does not date. What does date are the specifics — 1960s tax law, the Edsel, the English-pound crisis — so several essays drag if you have no patience for the period detail. It is uneven; two or three pieces are classics and a couple are skippable.

## Who should read it

Read it if you lead people and want to see how committees rationalize, how a smart company ships a disaster, and how individuals reason about ethics when the stakes are real. The narrative format makes it painless. Skip it if you want a checklist or a modern playbook — Brooks gives you neither, and that is on purpose.

## Where it earned its place

It mostly taught me a habit: distrust the tidy post-hoc story and look at what the people involved actually did, day to day. As pure reading it is a pleasure, which I cannot say about most of this shelf, and the Gates-Buffett endorsement is the rare blurb that earns itself.
