## The one-paragraph verdict

Prado is the highest-ranking paramilitary officer to write this kind of memoir, and when he's describing the actual work — the Contra war, running assets, building the early counterterrorism apparatus — it's specific and genuinely interesting. The problem recurring reviewers flag is real: the prose is workmanlike, and the book tilts hard toward self-justification, with the author always cast as the competent adult in the room. It reads less like history and more like a closing argument. Take the operational chapters at face value and treat the editorializing as the testimony of an interested party, and you get a useful, one-sided window into a closed world.

## Who should read it

Read it if you want the paramilitary, on-the-ground counterpart to the more polished analyst-and-station-chief memoirs — Prado's career sits in the rougher end of the trade. Skip it if you're looking for balanced history or critical distance on US covert action; this is a participant defending his life's work, not a historian weighing it.

## Where it earned its place

It's a clean reminder that primary sources are people with agendas, and that the most useful thing you can do with a confident narrator is separate what they did from how they want you to feel about it. Pure off-hours reading — but the habit of reading a source against its own framing carries over everywhere.
