## The one-paragraph verdict

Vance got the access nobody else did, and the reporting shows: the Falcon 1 launch failures, the 2008 cash crisis where both companies almost folded in the same week, the brutal pace inside the factories. Those passages are genuinely gripping and specific in a way most business biographies never manage. The weakness is distance — Vance keeps explaining away the human wreckage, and Musk's tyrannical streak gets softened into "the cost of greatness." You finish admiring the machine and distrusting the narrator a little. Still the best-reported account of the early years.

## Who should read it

Read it if you build things and want to see how hardware companies survive on willpower and luck more than plan. Engineers and founders will recognize the failure modes. Skip it if you want a balanced moral reckoning with the man — this is a 2015 portrait written largely on his terms, and it shows its age and its angle.

## Where it earned its place

It reset my sense of how thin the margin is between a "visionary" company and a dead one — months of runway, one launch, one bad quarter. That mindset stuck: ambition is cheap, surviving your own ambition is the hard part. I keep it on the shelf as a reminder of what relentless looks like up close, costs included.

## Skip it if…

You've already read Isaacson's later biography, or you want hagiography questioned rather than gently rationalized. Vance admires too much to push hard, and a decade on, plenty of the open questions he left have answers he couldn't see.
