## The one-paragraph verdict

Fox writes like a novelist, and the book moves the way good spy fiction does: tense, lonely, propulsive. The undercover-as-art-dealer premise carries real atmosphere, and the chapters on the cost of the work — the isolation, the constant performance — land honestly. But the same polish that makes it readable is also its weak point: scenes feel staged, details that should be verifiable stay vague, and several set-pieces strained my belief. The widely echoed criticism that it reads as embellished is fair. I finished it entertained, not informed.

## Who should read it

Read it if you want an atmospheric, character-driven espionage memoir and you're comfortable treating it as one person's heavily shaped account. It's a strong airport-and-couch book. Skip it if you're after a rigorous, documented look at how the CIA actually operates — this is memoir-as-mood, not reportage, and it won't satisfy anyone reading for accuracy.

## Where it earned its place

It gave me a few good evenings and a useful reminder: a confident first-person narrator is a powerful, and unreliable, thing. That instinct — to enjoy the story while keeping a skeptical eye on the claims — is worth carrying around, on and off the page.

## Skip it if…

Pass if unverifiable claims dressed as fact annoy you, or if you wanted tradecraft and history rather than a stylized personal arc. See the mixed reception on <ExternalCite title="Goodreads" url="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/50987995" /> before committing.
