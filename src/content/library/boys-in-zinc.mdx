## The one-paragraph verdict

This is testimony, not narrative: dozens of voices, lightly stitched, describing a war the Soviet state lied about while shipping its dead home in sealed zinc coffins. The recurring praise is right — the polyphonic method makes the grief specific and unbearable, and Alexievich never editorializes over the speakers. The recurring criticism is also fair: it is relentless, there is no arc to hold onto, and the controversy over how faithfully she shaped her interviewees' words (some later disputed their quotes in court) is worth knowing going in. I docked nothing for that, but it changes how you read "verbatim."

## Who should read it

Read it if you want history from the ground up — the texture of a collapse, not a summary of it. It rewards readers who already trust oral history and reportage. Skip it if you want analysis, timelines, or causes explained; this book refuses to do that work for you.

## Where it earned its place

It reset my bar for what "listening" means. The discipline of letting a source finish, unflattened, without rushing to a conclusion is something I carried back into incident write-ups and postmortems. Pure weight, no comfort — but I think about it often.

## Skip it if…

Pass if you're in a fragile place, or if you want a structured account of the war rather than its raw human residue. This book offers no relief and no thesis — only voices.
