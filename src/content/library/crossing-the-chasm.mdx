## The one-paragraph verdict

The central claim is sharp: between early adopters and the mainstream market there's a gap most technical products fall into, and you cross it by dominating one narrow beachhead instead of chasing everyone at once. That framing is worth the price on its own, and it explains a lot of stalled "we had early traction and then nothing" stories. The downside is that Moore says it early and then keeps saying it — the case studies are dated, the prose is repetitive, and the framework gets stretched over situations it doesn't quite fit. It's a long magazine article wearing a book's clothes. But the core mental model stuck, which is more than I can say for most go-to-market books.

## Who should read it

Read it if you're a founder or engineer who built something good and can't understand why the early enthusiasm didn't convert into a real market — that's the exact problem this book is about. PMs working the adopter-to-mainstream transition will get the vocabulary they're missing. Skip it if you're already past product-market fit, or if you want tactics rather than a model; this is a way of seeing, not a playbook.

## Where it earned its place

It gave me a single durable lens: pick one beachhead and own it before you generalize. I catch myself applying that to scope decisions, not just marketing — the instinct to win narrow before going wide is a good one to have wired in. That's a fair return for a short read.

## Skip it if…

If you don't have patience for repetition or 1990s tech anecdotes, the signal-to-padding ratio will annoy you. A solid summary captures most of the value in a fraction of the pages.
