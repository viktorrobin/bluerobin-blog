## The one-paragraph verdict

This is the MIT study that gave the world the term *lean* and made Toyota's production system legible to everyone outside Japan. The strength is the rigor: five years of comparative data across global auto plants, not a guru's hand-waving. The recurring praise — that it's unusually readable for a research report — holds up. The recurring criticism does too: it's a 1990 book steeped in automotive detail, repetitive in places, and some of its triumphalist predictions read as artifacts of their moment. You skim the assembly-line chapters and keep the operating model.

## Who should read it

Read it if you lead delivery and want the source text behind WIP limits, pull, and flow rather than a secondhand summary. Engineering and ops managers get the most out of it. If you only want the modern, software-shaped distillation, start with *The Phoenix Project* or *The Goal* and come back here later — or not at all.

## Where it earned its place

The one idea I actually used is queueing: large batches and high work-in-progress hide problems and stretch lead times. That's the spine of [Reducing WIP](/leadership/reducing-wip-finishing-beats-starting/) — limiting concurrent work so teams finish instead of starting, exactly the pull logic this book documents on the factory floor.

## Skip it if…

Skip it if you want a tactical playbook for software teams — this is industrial history and economics, not a sprint guide. The principles transfer; the examples need translation.
