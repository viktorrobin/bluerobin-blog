## The one-paragraph verdict

This was never written to be published — it's a Roman emperor's private journal, notes to himself, which is exactly why it lands. The recurring theme is brutally useful: separate what you control from what you don't, then drop the rest. The flip side, and reviewers say this constantly, is that it repeats itself and has no structure — the same handful of ideas circle back in slightly different words, with no argument building toward anything. I read the Gregory Hays translation and that matters; older translations turn plain Stoic advice into stiff Victorian English. Treat it as a notebook to dip into, not a book to read front-to-back, and the repetition stops being a flaw.

## Who should read it

Anyone who holds too much in their head at once — on-call engineers, people managers, anyone whose stress comes from things outside their control. It rewards short, repeated visits more than a single sitting. Skip it if you want a systematic philosophy text or a step-by-step argument; this is fragments, not a framework.

## Where it earned its place

No platform tie-in — this is purely off-duty reading. What it gave me is a habit: when something's going sideways, ask whether it's actually mine to fix, and if not, stop spending on it. That single move has quietly saved me more bad evenings than any productivity system.
