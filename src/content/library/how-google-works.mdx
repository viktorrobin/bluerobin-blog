The single idea — build around "smart creatives" and let the org follow — is worth the
read. Just don't expect it to teach you how to run a team that isn't already Google.
</Callout>

## The one-paragraph verdict

The premise lands: when leverage comes from a handful of high-agency people, your job is to
hire them, give them room, and stop managing them like cogs. Schmidt and Rosenberg are good
on hiring and on why culture is a design decision rather than a poster. The recurring, fair
criticism — which I share — is survivorship bias: it's the playbook of a company that had a
money-printing search business backing every bet, and a lot of the advice quietly assumes
that cushion. It's also more anecdote than framework, so you finish with conviction but few
repeatable moves. Read it for the mindset shift, not the operating manual.

## Who should read it

Managers and tech leads who set hiring bars and shape team culture, and anyone who wants the
inside rationalisation of Google's people decisions. Skip it if you're after concrete
systems for sizing, reorgs, or career ladders — this book gestures at those, it doesn't hand
you the tools.

## Where it earned its place

The "hire for the role you can't yet describe" instinct fed directly into how I think about
[sizing teams in the copilot era](/leadership/sizing-teams-copilot-era/): when each engineer
carries more leverage, you staff for judgement and range, not headcount. This book is where I
first took that argument seriously, even if it never does the staffing math itself. See
<ExternalCite title="How Google Works on Goodreads" url="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/23158207-how-google-works" />.

## Skip it if…

You want a tested method rather than a confident memoir. If your context isn't a cash-rich
platform company, read it with the survivorship caveat firmly in mind.
