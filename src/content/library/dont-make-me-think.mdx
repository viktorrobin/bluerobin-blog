## The one-paragraph verdict

Krug's core claim — every question a user has to stop and answer is a tax on your interface — has aged better than almost any design book I own. It's short, genuinely funny, and ruthless about cutting its own length, which is rare and welcome. The criticism is fair, though: several screenshots are visibly from the early-2000s web, and an experienced designer will find most of it obvious. I'm not a designer, so "obvious in hindsight" was exactly the point — it named patterns I was already violating. The chapter on running a five-dollar usability test alone earned the cover price.

## Who should read it

Read it if you're an engineer who inherited the frontend and has never been taught why your forms annoy people. Solo builders and first-time founders shipping their own UI get the most leverage per page. Career UX designers can skip it — they learned this years ago and will be bored.

## Where it earned its place

When I built the [glassmorphism design system](/articles/implementing-glassmorphism-design-system/) for Archives, Krug's "don't make me think" test was the filter I kept applying: every frosted panel had to survive the question "can someone tell what's clickable in half a second?" More than one pretty component lost that fight and got flattened. The book is why the system has visible affordances instead of just looking nice in a screenshot.

## Skip it if…

Skip it if you already do usability work professionally, or if you want depth on interaction design, accessibility internals, or research methodology. This is a first-principles primer, not a reference — its strength is brevity, which is also its ceiling.
