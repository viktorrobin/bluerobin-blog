## The one-paragraph verdict

In 1965 Robin Graham left California in a 24-foot sloop and came back five years and 33,000 miles later with a wife and a daughter. The book is surprisingly well written for someone that young — Derek Gill clearly earned his co-author credit — and its best move is refusing to drown you in navigation procedure; it stays on the person, not the rigging. The flip side is that the person isn't always likeable: there's a much-cited moment where Graham laments ocean pollution and then chucks a Coke bottle over the side, and that self-absorption surfaces more than once. It's an honest record of a teenager doing something extraordinary, flaws and all.

## Who should read it

Read it if you like first-person adventure memoirs and don't need a technical sailing manual underneath the story. The 1960s travel and the young romance carry it. Skip it if you came for seamanship detail — Graham deliberately leaves most of that out — or if a protagonist's adolescent blind spots will spoil the trip for you.

## Where it earned its place

This one's pure enjoyment, not platform research. It's a reminder that a big undertaking is mostly endured day by day by an ordinary, often flawed person — which is oddly steadying when a project feels too large to start. I keep it on the shelf as proof that "just leave the harbor" is sometimes the whole strategy.

## Skip it if…

You want a rigorous sailing narrative, or you have low tolerance for a young narrator's self-regard. The feat is real; the storyteller is still a teenager, and it shows.
