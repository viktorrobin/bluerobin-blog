## The one-paragraph verdict

The core idea earns its reputation: decide by category, not by room, hold each thing, and keep only what sparks joy. That single reframe — from "what can I throw away" to "what do I want to keep" — is a clean inversion of the default decision, and it actually works. The trouble is the book around it. It is repetitive; the client anecdotes blur into one template, and the same principles get restated past the point of usefulness. It also asks you to thank your socks and assumes your belongings have feelings, which is where a lot of readers (myself included) check out. There is maybe a long essay's worth of signal here, stretched to book length.

## Who should read it

Read it if your physical space is genuinely out of control and you respond well to a strict, one-size method that removes the agonizing. The category-by-category sequencing is good scaffolding. Skip it if you already declutter fine, or if anthropomorphized possessions and "your house has a spirit" framing will make you roll your eyes hard enough to miss the useful part.

## Where it earned its place

It gave me one durable habit: ask whether I actually want a thing, not whether I can justify keeping it. That flip transfers cleanly off the closet floor — to old code, to subscriptions, to half-finished side projects. I keep the book on the off-duty shelf as a reminder that a good heuristic survives a mediocre delivery.

## Skip it if…

Pass if you want evidence, nuance, or a method that adapts to your life rather than demanding you adapt to it. This is dogma, not a discussion — useful dogma, but take the heuristic and leave the ceremony.
