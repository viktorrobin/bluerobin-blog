## The one-paragraph verdict

The core idea is sound and underused: write a scorecard before you interview, define the outcomes the role must deliver, then run a structured "Topgrading" interview that walks the candidate chronologically through their career. The discipline of deciding what "good" looks like *before* you meet anyone is the whole point, and most teams skip it. Where the book wears thin is the relentless framing — the "A Player" branding, the 90%-success-rate claim that no one can reproduce, and a pace that stretches a long blog post into 200 pages. <ExternalCite title="Who on Goodreads" url="https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/4989687-who" /> reflects that split: people love the method and roll their eyes at the packaging.

## Who should read it

Engineering managers and founders who hire on instinct and keep getting burned — this gives you a repeatable process you can hand to a panel. If you already run scorecard-driven, structured interviews, you know everything here and can skip it.

## Where it earned its place

The scorecard-first discipline shaped how I wrote up on-call expectations in [on-call culture & hiring](/leadership/on-call-blamelessness-incident-culture-hiring/): defining the concrete outcomes a person must own *before* the loop, so "good in an incident" becomes testable instead of a vibe.

## Skip it if…

You want depth on assessing technical skill specifically — this is general-management hiring, light on engineering signal, and heavy on its own brand.
