## The one-paragraph verdict

Case and Deaton take a single grim statistic — rising mid-life mortality among less-educated white Americans — and trace it patiently through opioids, alcohol, suicide, lost jobs, and a healthcare system that bleeds the people it's meant to serve. The data work is excellent and hard to argue with; they refuse easy villains and let the numbers carry the case. Where it sags is the back third: the move from diagnosis to policy feels rushed, and the prescriptions (especially on healthcare cost) read more like sketches than the rigorous chapters before them. That mismatch is the most common complaint in reviews, and it's fair. Still worth the time for the diagnosis alone.

## Who should read it

Read it if you want to understand a real, measurable social collapse without ideological hand-waving — the authors are economists first and pundits a distant second. Skip it if you're after a policy manifesto; the "future of capitalism" in the title oversells what the closing chapters actually deliver. Not for anyone allergic to charts.

## Where it earned its place

This one earned its spot for the discipline of the argument: a habit of starting from a number you can't dispute and only then reaching for explanation. It's a good corrective to the instinct to lead with the story and find data to fit. I think about that ordering more than I expected to.

## Skip it if…

Pass if you already know the opioid-and-inequality story and want fresh solutions — the analysis is sharper than the answers, and you'll feel the gap.
