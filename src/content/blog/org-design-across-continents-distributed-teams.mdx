## The mistake

The change was a single field. A new "cancellation reason" on an order, surfaced in the UI, persisted, and emitted on the event that downstream billing already consumed. I estimated it at an afternoon.

It took eight days.

The field touched three teams. The Checkout team owned the screen. The Orders team owned the write path and the schema. The Payments team owned the event consumer and had opinions about the enum values. None of them owned the change, because the change didn't live inside any one of them — it lived in the seams between them.

So we did what you do. We scheduled a sync. Payments couldn't agree the enum without Orders, Orders didn't want to migrate the schema until Checkout confirmed the copy, and Checkout was waiting on the event shape. Three calendars, one decision, and a quiet week of "waiting to hear back from the other team."

I had built that org. I'd drawn the boundaries the convenient way — by who already reported to whom, by which manager had headroom, by which team was closest to a given screen. It looked tidy on the chart. It was the wrong shape for the system underneath it.

## What it taught me

The teams disagreed with the architecture, and when the org chart and the architecture disagree, the architecture wins — it just makes you pay in coordination. This is Conway's Law read backwards. Your systems come to mirror your communication structures, so if your communication structure cuts straight through a domain that wants to be one thing, every change across that cut needs a meeting.

I had treated team boundaries as an HR artifact. They are an architectural one. The boundary between two teams is a contract, whether you designed it or not, and an undesigned contract is the most expensive kind: implicit, contested, and renegotiated every time someone touches it.

The fix wasn't more synchronization. More meetings only redistribute the waiting. The fix was to move the boundaries so that the common changes stayed inside a single team — to let the domain decide the org, instead of the other way around.

<Callout type="info" title="The tell">
  If a routine change reliably needs three teams to agree, the change is trying to tell you where a team should be. Coordination cost is a map of your mismatched boundaries — read it.
</Callout>

## What I changed

I ran the reorg around the domain model rather than the reporting lines.

- **Drew teams around aggregates, not screens.** Orders, including its events and the rules that govern them, became one team's full responsibility — write path, schema, and the contract it emits. The "cancellation reason" change would now land inside one team's walls.
- **Made each seam an owned contract.** Every cross-team boundary got an explicit, typed interface with a single owning team. Changing the contract is a deliberate, versioned act; living behind it is nobody else's business.
- **Gave each team end-to-end ownership of its slice.** A team that owns a domain owns it from API to event to data, so it can ship most changes without a quorum from its neighbours.
- **Killed the standing cross-team syncs.** They were a symptom. Once the boundaries matched the domains, the meetings had nothing left to coordinate, and I deleted them rather than let them fill with new work.

<ImplementationNote>
  This is why BlueRobin makes its seams explicit on purpose. The domain aggregates and the typed contracts between them — the API surface and the NATS event shapes — aren't only an engineering convenience. They are the lines a team would map onto cleanly. When the model names the boundaries, the org chart has somewhere honest to follow.
</ImplementationNote>

## The results

The next change of that shape — another field, another reason code, threaded through write path and event — shipped in about a day, by one team, with no cross-team meeting. Decision latency on that class of work dropped from roughly a week to roughly a day.

The standing coordination meetings, three of them, went away. The people in them got the hours back, and the teams stopped describing their work as "blocked on another team," which had been the quiet refrain in every retro.

The honest caveat: this didn't make the genuinely cross-cutting decisions any cheaper. Changing a shared contract still costs real coordination, and it should — a versioned interface is exactly the kind of thing two teams ought to slow down and agree on. What the reorg fixed was the accidental coordination, the meetings we were holding because the boundaries were wrong. It did nothing for the essential kind, and a few large changes still need every team in the room.

If I were doing it again, I'd reorg sooner and smaller. I waited until the coordination tax was obvious to everyone, which meant the eventual move was bigger and more disruptive than a series of quiet boundary corrections along the way would have been.
