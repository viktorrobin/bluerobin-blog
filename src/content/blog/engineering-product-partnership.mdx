## The mistake

We called it the "Bulk Export" project, and for six weeks it was the cleanest thing my team had run all year. The ticket was precise: let admins export their account data as a configurable CSV, with column selection, date filters, and a progress bar for large jobs. We sized it, broke it down, and built it well. The progress bar was genuinely nice. We shipped on the day we said we would.

Three weeks later I pulled the usage numbers. A handful of accounts had touched it once. Almost no one came back.

I went looking for the bug and there wasn't one. The feature worked. The problem was that customers had never wanted a configurable CSV. They wanted to get their data *into a spreadsheet they already had*, and a raw export dropped them at the start of an hour of manual cleanup. What they were actually asking for, underneath the ticket, was a scheduled sync. Product knew customers were frustrated about "getting data out." Somewhere between that and the ticket, the real problem had been compressed into a spec, and my team had treated the spec as the truth.

That was the part that stung. We hadn't been sloppy. We had been excellent order-takers. Tickets came over the wall, we estimated and built them, and we measured ourselves on throughput. It felt cooperative. It produced a thing built right that solved the wrong problem, and not one engineer in the room had been in a position to say so.

## What it taught me

The healthiest engineering–product setup is not a clean handoff. It is shared ownership of the outcome.

Engineering that only executes cannot push back on the wrong problem, because it never sees the problem — only the solution someone already chose. And product that decides alone loses the feasibility instinct and the leverage that engineers can see from inside the system: the cheaper adjacent fix, the constraint that makes the elegant version impossible, the thing that is two days of work instead of two months.

The shift I needed was about influence. You earn a seat in defining the problem, not just in solving it, and you earn it by being useful upstream rather than by being fast downstream. Nobody hands that seat to a team that has only ever taken tickets.

<ImplementationNote>
  This is the same instinct I now design for in BlueRobin: features start from a real problem rather than a spec ([see the Archives](/the-archives/)). When a request lands, the first artifact is not a ticket but a short statement of the problem and the metric that would tell us we solved it. At team scale, that meant putting engineers in discovery — sitting in on the customer calls, reading the support threads — not just in delivery.
</ImplementationNote>

## What I changed

- I put two engineers into discovery for every meaningful initiative. Not to estimate — to help frame the problem and argue about the success metric before anyone wrote a ticket.
- I changed what we measured. Throughput stopped being the headline number. We started tracking whether shipped work moved the metric it was supposed to move, and we reviewed that together with product.
- We wrote one shared definition of done that included the outcome, not just the acceptance criteria. "Built and merged" no longer counted as finished on its own.
- I started a standing weekly forum where product and engineering traded context before commitments — constraints, leverage, known risk — so the cheap-adjacent-fix conversation happened before the roadmap hardened, not after.

<Callout type="info" title="The tell I watch for now">
  When an engineer can only describe *what* they're building and not *why it matters to a customer*, that is not an engineer problem. It is a sign the partnership has quietly slipped back into a handoff.
</Callout>

## The results

Over the next two quarters, scope churn — work we threw away or substantially reworked after the fact — dropped to roughly half of what it had been. The Bulk Export pattern stopped repeating: we caught two would-be tickets in discovery and reframed them before they cost a sprint.

Time from idea to something customers actually used got shorter, though more honestly than the dashboards suggested. Some initiatives took *longer* to start, because we argued about the problem first. They just failed far less often once they shipped.

The honest caveat: shared ownership blurred accountability for a while. When everyone owns the outcome, it gets murky who owns the call when product and engineering disagree. I had to draw an explicit line — engineering owns *how* and has a real voice in *what*, but product still owns the final prioritization decision. Co-ownership is not consensus, and pretending it was cost me a few slow, frustrating weeks before I named it.
