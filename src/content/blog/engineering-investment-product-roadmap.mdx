## The mistake

The intake service had a queue worker we called the "courier." Everyone knew it was held together with retries and hope. Twice that year an engineer had asked for a week to rebuild it, and twice I had said the honest thing: not this quarter, the launch comes first.

Each of those calls was defensible. A week of platform work versus a feature a customer was waiting on is not a hard decision in the moment. The feature wins. It almost always wins, because the feature has a name, a date, and someone asking about it, and the courier just has a quiet hum of risk.

So we shipped. And every quarter the courier got a little worse, because we kept routing new traffic through it without ever paying down the thing underneath.

Then, over one bad fortnight, it failed three times. Not dramatically the first time. A delayed batch, a manual replay, an apology. The third time it dropped messages we couldn't reconstruct, and the cleanup was no longer a week. It was most of a month, with two engineers off the roadmap entirely and me explaining to stakeholders why the thing they couldn't see had eaten the thing they could.

I had not avoided the cost. I had only removed my ability to choose when to pay it.

## What it taught me

The error wasn't any single deferral. It was treating engineering health as leftover capacity, the work that happens if a sprint comes in light. It never does. Leftover capacity is a rounding error you are quietly betting your reliability on.

The reframe I needed is that technical quality is a continuous investment, not a project you do once and finish. You are always either paying it down or letting it accrue, and "do nothing" is itself a position with a price. Once I held it that way, the question stopped being *should we do platform work or features* and became *what is the right sized, planned line for investment this quarter, and how do I argue for it.*

And the argument has to be in the language the roadmap already speaks: speed, reliability, cost. Not virtue, not craftsmanship, not "the team will be happier." Those may be true, but they don't survive a prioritisation meeting. "This cuts our incident recovery time roughly in half" does.

<Callout type="info" title="The reframe">
  A deferral is not a saving. It is a loan against future capacity, taken at an interest rate you don't control and can't see until it's due.
</Callout>

## What I changed

- I reserved a standing share of each cycle, around 15 to 20 percent, for engineering investment, and defended it like any other commitment instead of spending it first when a deadline slipped.
- I made every investment item carry a product outcome: faster delivery here, fewer pages there, lower cost over time. If I couldn't phrase it that way, it wasn't ready to argue for.
- I put the work on the *same* roadmap as features, sequenced and visible, not in a shadow backlog that only engineers ever saw.
- I split the large items so they could be delivered incrementally, route by route, rather than as the kind of all-at-once rewrite that had bitten us. The courier itself got migrated piece by piece behind the existing interface.
- I started naming the loan out loud. When we chose to defer, I said so explicitly and wrote down what we were borrowing against, so the deferral was a decision instead of a default.

## The results

Over the following two or three quarters, the courier-class incidents that had been eating whole weeks became rare, and the ones that remained were small enough to handle inside a normal day. Recovery time on the failures we did have dropped by roughly half, mostly because the systems were no longer mysteries.

Velocity is the part I'd describe carefully. We did not get dramatically faster, and I'd distrust anyone who claimed a clean before-and-after number here. What changed is that velocity got *steadier* — fewer quarters torched by an emergency nobody planned for. Predictability turned out to matter more to stakeholders than raw speed ever did.

The honest caveat: I overcorrected for about a quarter. Fresh from the cleanup, I let the investment line creep toward a third of capacity and started funding refactors that were aesthetic, not load-bearing. A reserved budget is a tool, not a virtue, and it can be misspent exactly like any other. The discipline isn't protecting the line. It's making each item earn its place on the same terms as a feature would.

If I were doing it again, I'd put numbers on the risk earlier. We had the incident data the whole time. I just hadn't turned it into the argument that would have let me pay the cost on a schedule of my choosing.
