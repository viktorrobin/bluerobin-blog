## The mistake

The proposal looked clean on the page. We were standing up a new service team, and the coding assistants had just become genuinely useful — the kind that draft a whole handler from a comment and get it mostly right. I did the arithmetic everyone was doing that quarter. If each engineer was suddenly producing more, I could staff the team at four instead of seven and still hit the roadmap. I wrote it down. I defended it in the planning review. I felt slightly clever.

Six weeks in, the team was generating more code than any group I had run, and shipping slower than the one it replaced.

The pull requests stacked up. Two of the four engineers were effectively full-time reviewers, because the volume of generated code that *looked* plausible was enormous and the cost of merging something subtly wrong was real. Nobody held the whole system in their head anymore — the assistant happily wrote into corners no human had read recently. Integration became a daily negotiation. The assistant had made drafting nearly free, and I had quietly assumed that drafting was the thing we were short on. It wasn't.

## What it taught me

The constraint on a team was never typing speed. It is coordination and the amount of system a group of people can hold in their heads at once. That is why the old rule of thumb — that a team lands somewhere around six to eight people — survives contact with tooling that triples raw output. The number tracks human bandwidth, not keystrokes.

What the assistants actually did was move the bottleneck. When drafting is cheap, the scarce work becomes reviewing, specifying, and verifying — deciding what should exist, and confirming that what got generated is correct. Output went up; the ability to *trust* output did not come along for free. Cutting the team in half did not remove a bottleneck. It removed the people who were supposed to absorb the new one.

  Cheap generation doesn't shrink a team's work, it relocates it. The hours you save on drafting reappear, larger, as review, specification, and verification. Size for that.
</Callout>

## What I changed

- **I stopped sizing teams against output and started sizing them against review and shared context.** Teams stayed in the six-to-eight band. "AI means we can run this at half headcount" became a claim I made people defend with evidence, not a default.

- **I moved the freed-up capacity into the new constraint** instead of banking it as a saving. Time the assistants gave back went into tests, evaluation harnesses, and the review pipeline — the work that lets you trust a higher volume of generated code.

- **I made specification a first-class activity.** When drafting is free, the expensive mistake is building the wrong thing quickly. We invested more in writing down what a change should do before generating it, which made review a comparison against intent rather than a guess.

- **I changed what I measured.** Lines and raw throughput went into the bin. We watched review latency, the rework rate as generated volume rose, and a rough self-reported load signal per engineer. Those are the numbers that move when a team is past its real limit.

## The results

Over the following two quarters, review latency on that team fell by roughly half — pull requests that had sat for two or three days were getting a real read inside a day. The rework rate, which had been climbing as generated volume rose, flattened and then drifted down as the eval and test scaffolding caught the plausible-but-wrong changes earlier. Throughput per person was modestly up, but the honest framing is that it was up because the team was the *right* size to absorb the new work, not because four people had become seven.

The caveat: AI genuinely did change the headcount math in one direction I underweighted. For well-bounded, well-specified work with strong tests around it, a slightly smaller team really can carry more, because the verification cost is low and the assistant's output is easy to trust. Where it did *not* help — and where I was simply wrong — was anywhere the system was poorly understood or the cost of a subtle error was high. There, more generation made things worse, faster. The lesson wasn't "teams stay the same size." It was that the size is set by how much you have to verify, and you have to know which kind of work you're staffing.

If I were doing it again, I would have built the eval and review capacity *before* touching the headcount, not in a scramble afterward.
