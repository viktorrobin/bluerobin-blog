## The mistake

We called it Project Lighthouse. It was the kind of feature that looks tidy on a slide: a new customer-facing workflow that needed a contract change in the platform team, a schema migration from the data team, a UI surface from the product team, and a billing hook from a fourth team. Four teams, one feature, one date. I drew the date on the roadmap myself.

The plan looked sound. Each team gave me an estimate, I lined them up against a shared launch day about ten weeks out, and we agreed to integrate everything in the final sprint. On the wall it was a clean Gantt chart with four bars finishing neatly together.

For eight weeks it felt fine. Standups were green. Demos showed each team's piece working in isolation. I remember telling my own manager that Lighthouse was "on track," and believing it.

Then we tried to put the pieces together. The platform contract had quietly changed shape — a field the UI team assumed was a string had become a typed object. The billing hook depended on an event the data migration hadn't started emitting yet. None of these were dramatic failures. They were small mismatches at the boundaries, and there were a dozen of them, and we found them all in the same week.

We slipped about three weeks. Worse, I'd had no warning. Everything was green right up until it wasn't, because we measured each team against its own bar and never measured the thing that actually mattered: whether the pieces fit.

## What it taught me

Cross-team work rarely fails inside a team. It fails at the seams between them — the contracts, the handoffs, the assumptions each team makes about what the others will hand over. So the job isn't to manage four teams. It's to manage the seams.

Two changes follow from that. First, make the dependencies explicit before you commit to anything. A dependency you can name is a dependency you can sequence; the ones that hurt are the ones nobody wrote down. Second, sequence the work into increments that each integrate and deliver something verifiable — small, real merges across team boundaries, early and often — rather than saving integration for the end.

This is program sequencing, and it is the same incremental-over-big-bang logic we apply to a careful migration, pointed at delivery instead of code. Aligning teams on the *increments* and the dependency order beats aligning them on a shared finish line. A deadline tells everyone when to be done. It tells no one whether their work will actually meet the next team's.

<Callout type="info" title="Green is not a signal until it integrates">
  A team reporting "done in isolation" is reporting on the easy part. The only status worth trusting on cross-team work is whether two pieces have actually been merged and exercised together. Until then, green means "no news," not "good news."
</Callout>

## What I changed

- I stopped committing to a date before mapping the dependency graph. Every cross-team feature now starts with the teams in a room naming who needs what from whom, and in what order, written down where everyone can see it.
- I sequenced the work into milestones that each cross at least one team boundary and produce something demoable. The first milestone is deliberately the riskiest seam, not the easiest feature.
- I made the contracts concrete early — a typed interface, a sample payload, a stub endpoint — so the assumption that bit us on Lighthouse gets tested in week two instead of week nine.
- I replaced the single launch date on the tracker with the milestone sequence and its dependency edges, so "are we on track" became a question about integrated slices, not isolated bars.
- I gave each milestone an owner who sits on the seam, not inside one team, and whose only job is to confirm the pieces meet.

<ImplementationNote>
  BlueRobin's architecture makes this concrete: typed contracts between services and [the system topology](/architecture/) are the seams, drawn on purpose. When a service boundary is an explicit, versioned contract rather than a shared assumption, the dependency graph is already visible — you just have to walk it in increments. The org version of the lesson is the same as the engineering one: name the seams, then deliver across them a slice at a time.
</ImplementationNote>

## The results

The next comparable program ran with this approach end to end.

- Slip against plan fell from the roughly three weeks on Lighthouse to a few days on the following feature of similar size.
- Late-integration defects — the boundary mismatches that surface only when pieces meet — dropped by something like half, because we were meeting at the boundaries every week instead of once.
- The status was honest earlier. We caught the first real dependency miss in week two and absorbed it, where before it would have hidden until the final sprint.

The honest caveat: this has real overhead. Mapping dependencies and standing up integrated milestones costs days of coordination before any feature code ships, and for a small change touching two teams it is plainly not worth it — I've watched the ceremony outweigh the risk on work that a single shared channel would have handled. The method earns its keep when the dependency count is high enough that the seams are where the danger lives. Knowing which features clear that bar is still a judgment call, and I get it wrong sometimes in both directions.
